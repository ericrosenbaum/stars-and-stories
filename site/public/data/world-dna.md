Between September 2024 and July 2026 — from age two years and one month to three years and ten — Izzy and her dad recorded 193 improvised bedtime stories: roughly 219,000 words of trains, mice, moles, potions, and puking cats, told in the dark under a star projector. That happens to be the exact window in which developmental science says a storyteller is born. So we read every word of every transcript, counted everything countable, and took seven questions from the researchers who study how children learn to tell stories. Here is what the universe said back.

---

### 1. WHO'S DRIVING THIS STORY?

Ever since psychologists Wood, Bruner, and Ross coined the term **scaffolding** in 1976, the definition has come to include the dismantling: support only counts as scaffolding if it fades and hands responsibility over as competence grows. The corpus is a time-lapse film of exactly that. At 2;1, Dad asks every question and Izzy fills the slots ("*It was driving to Monica.*" — "To Monica? Who's that?" — "*No... Nana's house.*"). By 2;7 she has invented her signature power move, deployed across eighteen separate stories: "*Keep telling the story and then you'll find out*" — a way to hold the steering wheel without having to know the road. At 3;7 the handover is total, and hilarious: "*Once upon a time. Now repeat.*" She dictates; Dad recites. By 3;9 she can even explain the casting: "*Actually, I think you are the one because you're very good at Seeker and I'm not very good because I have a life of stuff you know.*" And here's the twist the research predicts (Haden, Haine & Fivush, 1997: parents' narrative structuring *increases* across the preschool years): Dad never stops scaffolding — his support just moves up a level, from finishing her sentences to maintaining the potion closet, the going-home potions, and the physics of an entire cosmos.

---

### 2. THE LADDER OF ONCE-UPON-A-TIME

Arthur Applebee mapped how children climb from "heaps" of unrelated events to true plotted narratives. Izzy climbs the whole ladder on tape. At 2;4 she discovers the load-bearing beam of all fiction: "*The problem was his bookcase was broken and it had a hole in it.*" At 2;6 she litigates story grammar itself: "*No, that's not the beginning! The beginning is she said 'Hmm, I'm going to build a tall tower.'*" And at exactly 3;0 — the very week of her birthday — "once upon a time" enters her own mouth for the first time, and out comes a complete, self-contained story: "*Once upon a time there was a mouse named Mickey. He was away from her house. She had a big adventure so she didn't have to worry. The end.*" Beginning, middle, emotional stakes, resolution — twenty-six words. The numbers climb with her: her average turn more than doubles across the corpus (about five words at two, thirteen at three and a half), and by 3;10 she deploys the professional's flourish: "*There was only one little problem.*"

---

### 3. VOICES IN HER MOUTH

Quoted speech is late-blooming, cognitively expensive stuff — you must hold another mind inside yours and let it talk. Izzy's first dialogue tags appear at 2;4 ("*You could! Of course! said his dad*"), and from there the corpus fills with other minds. She translates for creatures who can't speak ("*Meow meow marbel... That means I got my claw stuck*"). At 3;4 she achieves full narrative inception: as Mrs. Piggy, she tells Seeker a story about fighting with her sister — a story within a story within a bedtime, with a stated moral ("*the happy ending is when I got to play with my toy again that was broken, that my sister broke*"). By 3;10 she is narrating genealogy like a bard: "*It was about Hattie's great-great-great-great-grandmother, who was also named Hattie, but she was Ancient Hattie the Mouse. And she lived a very busy life, dodging the dinosaurs...*"

---

### 4. IS THIS A PARACOSM? (VERDICT: EMPHATICALLY)

Cohen and MacKeith named the **paracosm** — a private imagined world with persistent characters, geography, and laws, maintained over months or years — and documented it mostly in children seven and up. This corpus shows one assembling itself at three. The laws are explicit, and Izzy states them like a physicist: "*Potions are something you drink and then you say a rhyme and then they teleport you somewhere. That's how they work.*" The canon is defended with the zeal of a copyright lawyer: "*No, not blue fur, orange fur*" (Frasgle's coloring, non-negotiable); "*No, made by Isabella. Silver Cat can't make potions*" (magical labor law). The geography splices real and imagined — the flight path to the Planet of the Mookers runs down actual Cornell Street — and the continuity is astonishing: Hattie the Mouse has now run her workshop for sixteen unbroken months, and story arcs (a kitten's lost mother) span three separate nights. Root-Bernstein's research ties exactly this kind of childhood "worldplay" to adult creativity. The file is thick and still growing.

---

### 5. THE WORD FORGE, OR WHY MAGIC MUST RHYME

Kornei Chukovsky, who spent decades collecting toddler speech, called the young child "a linguistic genius" driven by an appetite for rhyme. In this universe that appetite got promoted to a law of nature: magic *only works if you say a rhyme*, so the entire cosmos runs on Izzy's phonological play — "*Higher and higher to the plane of fire*," "*Kamoose-kajuice-kaboose.*" You can watch the forge working live at 3;5: "*Siffle Piffle. I just made up a rhyme. Siffle Piffle Pip... No, Siffle Piffle Pup... No, Sniffle... No, Siffle Papiffle.*" She knows precisely what she's doing — at 2;7 she coined "brio neo" and announced, deadpan, "*That means nothing!*" — and at 3;3 she pitched a coinage in-character and got it canonized: "*And Seeker said, 'And if you want to make a new rhyme, I have one in mind that I'd like to use: mooska-kadooska,' said Seeker.*" Bissolam, Frasgle, snergle, squerret, Floofer Kaploofer: a dictionary no one else will ever need, and everyone here needs nightly.

---

### 6. LIFE, MYTHOLOGIZED

Susan Engel's great thesis is that in constructing stories, children are constructing themselves; Katherine Nelson's *Narratives from the Crib* showed toddlers doing that work alone in the dark before sleep. This corpus is the two-player version, and nothing real is wasted. A baby sister's name is ratified by a skeptical toy rabbit. Potty training enters the saga as "potty suits." The family cats' digestive tragedies become an anthem — 90% Izzy-composed, with genuine forensic puzzlement: "*I'm confused because they don't puke in their litter box.*" A fossil kit becomes the Prehistoric Potion; a sibling toy-fight is processed through Mrs. Piggy; and frustration itself gets narrated in real time: "*I got to get my silly out. I got to get my tears out, and my energy out, and everything out.*" Sometimes the myth-making goes strangely profound. Once, aged 2;11, gazing at the ceiling stars: "*I saw that the emotions are ocean.*"

---

### 7. THE SOFT LANDING

Vivian Gussin Paley spent a career showing that story *is* the child's instrument for thinking through fear. Here, the instrument is played from both ends of the bed. At 2;6 Izzy names the fear and boards the submarine anyway: "*To the deep-deep dark. But I'm a little bit scared of the deep-deep dark.*" The coping scripts she's given come back out through her characters — "*Take a deep breath and count to four*"; "*But don't be really mad Hattie. All of your feelings are okay*"; later her own invention, "*Stomp and bounce and squeak out loud!*" It doesn't always work — one night the frame collapses entirely ("*But I want to do beads. I'm disappointed. I just want to do beads*") — but the trend line bends toward tenderness: by 3;10 she is the one singing Dad-as-Seeker a lullaby ("*New places are hard. When you go to sleep, it feels more better, when you tell me why*") and theorizing which stories are safe for sleeping. Every voyage, however far, folds back into pajamas and blankets. Or as the oldest star in Star World put it, when asked for its wisdom: *just keep... glowing.*
