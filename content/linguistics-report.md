# The Languages of the Stars & Stories Universe

*A preliminary descriptive survey, compiled from the Stars & Stories transcript archive:
208 improvised bedtime stories, 2024-09-20 → 2026-08-29, 16,948 conversational turns,
258,901 words (31.9% Izzy). Every form cited below is quoted verbatim from a transcript.
Claims that go beyond the transcripts are marked **[inference]** or **[speculation]**.
Part IX lists what the archive does not say.*

---

## Abstract

The Stars & Stories cosmos supports at least seventeen distinct communicative codes, of
which English is only the matrix. This paper sorts them into three classes — the
**zoosemiotic languages** (§3), spoken by animals and small creatures and built from a
single reduplicated syllable; the **mechanical and alien languages** (§4), spoken by
ladders, robots, rocks and one unclassifiable being called Dit Darnet; and the
**incantation register** (§5), which is not anyone's native language but a ritual code
whose correct use is a law of physics. We show that the zoosemiotic languages are
holophrastic and radically context-resolved, that utterance length does *not* map
monotonically onto propositional content, and that the incantation register has a
genuinely compositional syntax that a character states outright and then demonstrates
with a minimal pair. We close with the archive's central unresolved question, which is
whether the sign is arbitrary — a matter on which the corpus contains explicit and
irreconcilable testimony from the same speaker.

---

## I. Method, and a caution about the record

The corpus is **oral**. Every text in the archive is a transcription of a recording made
in the dark, and no speaker in it has ever seen these words written down. This has one
consequence the reader should keep in front of them throughout: **there is no
orthographic standard, and apparent spelling variation is evidence about the
transcription, not about the language.**

The archive spells one dragonet's name *Dimitar, Dimatar, Dimetar, Demitar, Dematar* and
*Dibbletarr*; the cat school is *Catasory, Catasorry, Ginger Catasorry* and *Cadasori*;
*Frasgle* is also *Frazgle*; *Beaker* is also *Beeker*; *Grisco* is also *Griscko* and
*Grisko*; *Hattie* was *Haddy* for the archive's first eight months. None of this is
dialect variation. It is a transcriber — sometimes a human, sometimes an ASR engine —
guessing at a sound.

Where this paper gives a phonological argument, it therefore rests on **rhyme, syllable
count and reduplication**, which survive transcription, and not on segmental detail,
which does not.

A second caution: the archive's two speakers are a father and a daughter aged 2;1 to
3;10, and much of what is presented below as "canon" was invented seconds before it was
uttered. The languages are nonetheless internally consistent to a degree that repays
description, and the inconsistencies (§IX) are as interesting as the regularities.

## II. The linguistic ecology

Seventeen codes are attested. The archive names a language in two competing ways: as a
**bare stem** ("I speak duck", "I speak cat", "I do not speak buzz", "he can only meep")
or as a **stem plus the classifier *language*** ("meep language", "cat language", "ladder
language", "in elephant language"). Exactly one language in the entire corpus receives a
derived, English-style glottonym — see Ladderish, §4.1.

scribal ghost born of a bilabial confusion is, appropriately, §IX's first open question.

## III. The zoosemiotic languages

### 3.1 Phonology is determined by the body

The archive states the governing principle outright, in a scene where the mouse Tot asks
Izzy what language her invented words belong to:

> **Tot:** "What kind of language is that, Isabella?"
> **Izzy:** "Not any kind. It's just made-up words."
> **Dad (as Tot):** "Oh, I like made-up words, like squeakety-squeak and squork. […]
> most of my made-up words are just squeaks **'cause I'm a mouse**."
> — *The Stinky Cheese Volcano and the Floating Balloon Sky*, 2026-07-21

This is the corpus's theory of articulatory phonetics in one line. A speaker's inventory
of possible new words is bounded by the body that makes them: a mouse's coinages are
squeaks not because mice lack imagination but because mice are mice. It explains the
otherwise striking fact that every zoosemiotic language in the table above is built from
**one syllable, reduplicated** — *meep, blub, mook, meow, squeak, bzz, bark* — while the
mechanical languages (§4) are not.

Note also that Izzy immediately corrects Dad's coinage — he offers *squeakety-squeak*,
she returns *Squackety-squeak* — which is the archive's editorial pattern in miniature and
is discussed in §7.2.

### 3.2 Length does not encode content

The obvious hypothesis — that a longer utterance carries more meaning — is stated by no
one and refuted by the data. Consider the range:

| Utterance | Length | Gloss given in the text | Gloss length |
|---|---|---|---|
| *"Meep!"* | 1 syll. | "he loves chocolate and cherry and icing and frosting cakes with cherries on top" | 13 words |
| *"Meep!"* | 1 syll. | "thank you" | 2 words |
| *"Squeak, squeak!"* | 2 syll. | "Hello, who's there?" | 3 words |
| *"Meow"* ×7 | 7 syll. | "Excuse me, I wonder if you could help us. We're looking for a lost ball." | 15 words |
| *"Meow"* ×47 (in three turns) | 47 syll. | "she's lost in what work she wants" | 7 words |
| *"Meep"* ×13 | 13 syll. | a 60-word speech about adopting a family of bunnies | 60 words |
| [Roar] | 1 | "yes" | 1 word |
| [Loud long roar/shriek] | 1 | "No thank you, I don't like dust baths." | 8 words |

One *meep* can carry thirteen words; forty-seven *meows* carry seven. The correlation is
not merely weak, it is inverted at the extremes. **[inference]** The reduplication is
therefore best analysed as **prosodic and affective rather than segmental** — it encodes
insistence, agitation or duration of feeling, not lexical material. Ginger's
twenty-nine-*meow* turn is not a long sentence; it is a cat who cannot decide what to do
today, at length.

Beaker's distress form supports this. Under stress the shape changes rather than merely
lengthening: after falling in the ocean he produces *"Me, me, me, me, me, me, meep"* and
*"Me, me, me, me, meep, me, me, me, me, meep"* — a run of reduced open syllables closed
by a full *meep* coda. The character record independently describes him as communicating
"through a frantic cadence of staccato 'meeps' and 'me-me' sounds." **[inference]** Meep
has at least two registers, neutral and agitated, distinguished by syllable reduction and
codas rather than by segments.

### 3.3 Holophrasis and pragmatic resolution

The same form recurs with wildly different glosses. Frasgle's unmarked [Roar] is
translated, within a single story, as "yes", "okay", "okay, I'll try that", "that's an
interesting idea", and "you're right". The blub creature Blubbert's eighteen-*blub* turn
and his four-*blub* turn are handled by the same interpreter without distinction.

**[inference]** These languages are **holophrastic** — one form, one whole speech act —
and the specific proposition is supplied almost entirely by context and by the
interpreter's judgement. The languages carry illocutionary force (assent, complaint,
greeting, distress) reliably; propositional content is reconstructed, not transmitted.

This is why translation in the archive is *hedged* wherever the interpreter is a novice:

> Minnie: "Wait a minute. **I think** I'm starting to understand Frasgle. **I think** that
> one meant 'Yeah, okay, I'd like to have a bath too.'" And Mickey said, "**I think** that
> means you're right."
> — *Frasgle and the Sticky Situation*, 2025-07-31

Three hedges in two sentences. By contrast Bunsen, Beaker's established interpreter of
long standing, never hedges — he simply reports. **[inference]** Hedging in this corpus
tracks the interpreter's authority, not the language's difficulty.

### 3.4 Frasglish has phonemic contrasts, and they are enforced

The single strongest piece of evidence that these are *languages* and not merely noises
is a correction:

> Mole said, "Oh my, I've never met a creature like you. Now, I wonder if I could try to
> speak your language." And so you know what Mole said? [Small roar/grunt]. **Which wasn't
> quite right. And so Frasgle corrected Mole.** He said, [Roar].
> — *Frasgle and the Sticky Situation*, 2025-07-31

A learner produces an approximation; a native speaker judges it ill-formed and supplies
the correct token. That is a phonemic contrast being policed. Whatever separates
[Small roar/grunt] from [Roar] is **contrastive in Frasglish**, and the transcription
cannot tell us what it is.

### 3.5 Borrowing, observed live

The same story contains what is, as far as this survey can determine, the only documented
case of **lexical borrowing in progress** anywhere in the archive. Izzy, an English
speaker, offers Frasgle a novel form as a gift:

> **Izzy:** "Frasgle, I have a noise for you: Zararar, Zararar!"
> **Dad:** And then Frasgle did something new. That really caught Frasgle's attention. And
> you know what he said this time? **Zararar roar!** Zararar roar! And then Mole turned to
> Isabella and said, "Isabella, you got Frasgle to say something different! But what does
> that mean?"
> **Izzy:** "It means… I love you!"
> **Dad:** "Zararar roar!" said the Mole, and Frasgle said it back: "Zararar roar!" And now
> everyone said I love you to each other.

Every stage of a loan is here in nine lines: introduction by an outsider; adoption by the
recipient; **compounding with native material** (*Zararar* + *roar*, the borrowed stem
taking a Frasglish head); semantic assignment by the donor; and community-wide uptake, as
Mole and then "everyone" adopt it. The new item does not replace anything — Frasglish had
no attested way to say *I love you* before this — so it fills a genuine lexical gap.

**[speculation]** *Zararar* is phonologically unlike anything else Izzy coins. Its
closest relative in the entire corpus is *ar, zar, zar*, the sole attested Ladderish
(§4.1), recorded eight months later. Two unrelated non-human codes sharing a rare
*zar*-shape is either coincidence, a substrate this survey cannot reach, or evidence that
Izzy has a stable phonaesthetic category for "alien speech." The archive does not decide.

### 3.6 Relay interpretation, and receptive competence

The most recent story in the archive, recorded on its final day, contains the most
sophisticated linguistic architecture in it. Beaker announces a discovery:

> "We have made an incredible discovery, which is that I am able to **translate blub
> language into meep language**. And then Bunsen translates meep language into English."
> — *The Secret of the Galapagos Blub Creatures*, 2026-08-29

This is a **relay chain**: blub → meep → English, with no direct blub–English channel
anywhere. It has real consequences in the story — when Blubbert finally speaks, Bunsen
notes "It's a process here to translate" before delivering the gloss, and the latency is
played for exactly what it is.

The story also draws, precisely, the distinction between **receptive and productive**
bilingualism:

> "Beaker has learned how to speak the language of, or at least **understand** it. **He
> can't speak blub just yet.**" […] "He can only meep, not blub."

Beaker comprehends blub and produces only meep. This is the ordinary condition of the
heritage bilingual, and the archive gets it right, including the hopeful *yet*.

### 3.7 Literacy: meep has no script

The same story establishes that meep is written only by proxy:

> **Dad:** "But Beaker only knows meep language."
> **Izzy:** "No, no. Bunsen helped Beaker write it. **Beaker just told him the words in
> meep.** […] and then Bunsen wrote them."

Beaker composes in meep; Bunsen renders it in English orthography. **[inference]** None of
the zoosemiotic languages has a native writing system. Every written message in the
archive is in English, and every non-English speaker who wants to write must go through a
bilingual scribe. This is a well-attested real-world sociolinguistic pattern and it
arrived here without anyone planning it.

## IV. The mechanical and alien languages

### 4.1 Ladderish, and the problem it creates

The magic flying ladder speaks. Its language is named twice, in two different ways, in
consecutive turns:

> **Izzy:** "It talks the **ar, zar, zar** language."
> **Dad:** "Also known as **Ladderish**."
> — *The Case of the Kidnapped Dwarf and the Magic Ladder*, 2026-08-16

*Ladderish* is the only glottonym in the corpus formed with the English language-name
suffix *-ish*. Every other language is named by its own noise (*meep*, *blub*, *mook*) or
by its speakers (*cat language*, *elephant language*). Ladderish is the one code the
characters have bothered to name **the way we name languages**.

It also creates the deepest theoretical problem in the archive. Eight months earlier, the
same ladder cast a spell:

> And so they all said the rhyme together, **except the ladder, who said the rhyme in
> ladder language**, and then pring, poof, they appeared flying through the sky.
> — *The Giant Rescue on Volcano Island*, 2025-12-07

**The spell worked in translation.** This is not a small detail. §5 will show that
incantations in this universe are governed by a strict formal requirement — they must
rhyme — and yet here a caster produces the spell in a language whose only attested
material is *ar, zar, zar*, and the party teleports normally. Either Ladderish rhymes in
ways we cannot see, or magical efficacy is **semantic rather than phonetic**: what matters
is that the caster meant the spell, not that particular sounds left a particular mouth.
The archive is silent, and §IX returns to it.

### 4.2 Nyerber-nyerber

Dit Darnet appears in exactly one story and is gone. His language is disyllabic and
reduplicating like the zoosemiotic codes, but he is no animal — he is an engineer who
repairs a marble run — and Dad flags him as a foreigner rather than a beast:

> "Dit Darnet says, nyerber nyerber nyerber. Oh, **Dit Darnet doesn't speak our
> language.** He speaks nyerber-nyerber."
> — *Blast Off to the Hundred Acre Wood*, 2025-08-14

Two things are notable. First, *"our language"* — the only place in the corpus where the
English speakers frame themselves as a speech community with an outside. Second, the
scene contains the archive's only **explicit offer of language instruction**:

> **Dad:** "Nyerber nyerber nyerber. He said thank you. **I speak nyerber-nyerber. Do you
> want to try?**"
> **Izzy:** "No."

She declines. She does, however, use it unprompted moments later — *"He he nyerber nyerber
nyerber? That's the wire plug and that's the battery"* — code-switching into nyerber to
put the question and then glossing herself in English. She refuses the lesson and speaks
the language anyway, which is a fair summary of how she acquires everything else in this
archive.

### 4.3 The mookers, and a root that will not stay a noun

Mooker is the most morphologically productive non-English root in the corpus. From *mook*
the archive derives, without comment:

- **species noun** — *the Mookers*
- **language name** — *"mooker language"*
- **utterance** — *"Mook, mook, mook"*
- **place name** — *the Planet of the Mookers*, *the mooker cloud*
- **game** — *mookball*, *the Solar Mookball*
- **verb** — and here the archive does something genuinely odd:

> the Mookers were all tired out from their big day of playing with the Floofers, and so
> they decided to **mook their way right into bed** and **mook right to sleep**.
> — *The Mookers, the Floofers and the Solar Mookball*, 2026-03-28

*To mook* is a verb of motion in the first instance and something like an aspectual
auxiliary in the second — *mook right to sleep* on the model of *drift right to sleep*.
**[inference]** For the Mookers, *mook* is not a word in their language; it is the
language, functioning as an all-purpose root that takes whatever category the sentence
needs. Compare *smurf*.

Dad claims partial competence — "**I do speak a little bit of mooker language myself.**
Mook, mook, mook. And that means I hope I can help you out" — and glosses three syllables
as seven words, consistent with §3.2.

### 4.4 Robot

Zigzag the robot is commanded to produce language, and what emerges is not a reduplicated
animal syllable but a **compound of a machine noise and an English word**:

> "Okay, robot, say a made-up word." And the robot said, "**bleep-bloop,
> flibbertigibbet**."
> — *Zigzag the Dancing Robot and the Cornebyborn Dance*, 2025-10-31

*Flibbertigibbet* is a real English word, and Zigzag is the only speaker in the archive to
use it. **[speculation]** Robot speech is the corpus's one attested case of a code that is
*lexically parasitic* — it takes its material from English rather than from a body — which
would follow neatly from §3.1's principle: a machine has no species-specific articulatory
inventory to draw on, so it borrows.

### 4.5 The crying rocks

Space rock language is attested once, as *"Waaaaaa!"*, repeated identically when Dad asks
for a repetition. Its character record reads: "Sentient, crying entities found floating in
space that speak their own unique language." Dad cannot translate it and neither can Izzy;
it is the one language in the corpus that goes **entirely unglossed**. It is also the only
one whose single attested utterance is indistinguishable from crying.

## V. The incantation register

The incantation register is not anyone's mother tongue. No one is a native speaker of it;
it has no community; it is used only to do things. It is nonetheless the most thoroughly
described code in the archive, because in this universe **its grammar is a law of
physics**, and physics gets explained to children.

### 5.1 The rhyme requirement

Izzy states the mechanism directly, quoted in the archive's own analysis notes:
"*Potions are something you drink and then you say a rhyme and then they teleport you
somewhere. That's how they work.*" The teacher Ove states the constraint as a rule of the
art:

> "**magic spells must rhyme.** But each time you cast one, **you can make up a new rhyme
> if you like.**"
> — *The Magic of Mooska-Kadooska*, 2025-11-27

Both halves matter. Rhyme is **obligatory**; the particular rhyme is **free**. This is a
formal requirement on the shape of an utterance with no requirement on its content — a
combination with few real-world parallels outside verse forms and, notably, no parallel at
all in ordinary language, where form is constrained by what you mean. Here you may mean
anything you like so long as it chimes.

### 5.2 Three architectures

The 26 incantations recovered from the corpus fall into three structurally distinct types.

**Type A — opaque monoliths.** Wholly non-English strings with no internal structure the
speakers can name. *Ruby Dooby Galoob* (2025-11-28), *Izza Ka Pizza* (2026-02-21),
*Comoose* (2026-07-13), *Subime* (2026-06-22), *Za bing, zong, zing* (2026-08-29),
*Kamoose-kajuice-kaboose* (2026-07-06), *Zip-sip* (2026-07-06), *Salime, sume, sime, sume,
bime* (2026-07-19), *Ding-dong cadong-dong* (2026-06-15), *Aska-baska-noona-maska*
(2025-08-06), *Slime-ka-by, slime-ka-by, byme* (2026-06-23).

**Type B — bipartite: free invocation + English imperative.** This is the school register,
taught by Ove, and the only spell type in the archive whose semantics are demonstrably
compositional. See §5.4.

**Type C — transparent English couplets naming the destination.**

> "to go to the plane of fire, we must all close our eyes and speak the chant: **'Higher
> and higher to the plane of fire.'**"
> — *The Mischief Moles and the Plane of Fire*, 2025-10-07

> "Planet of the Mookers, we're the Blue-kers." (×3)
> — *Wobbly Whisker's Sticky Adventure on Planet Mooker*, 2026-05-02

Type C spells *say where they are going*. They satisfy the rhyme requirement in plain
English and are the only incantations whose meaning any English speaker could recover
unaided. **[inference]** Type C looks like the register's honest core, with Types A and B
as elaborations — but the chronology refuses to cooperate. The unambiguous first
attestations run Type A (2025-07-19), Type C (2025-10-07), Type B (2025-11-27), and the
two earliest spells of all (*Junk and gunk!*, 2025-03-11; *Mouse jouse*, 2025-03-12) are
English rhyming pairs that transform an object without naming a destination or supplying
an imperative — a shape that never recurs. The register did not evolve from transparency
toward opacity or the reverse. All three types coexist to the last recording.

### 5.3 Morphology: the KA-infix

The single most productive morphological process in the corpus is the insertion of a
**-ka-** element between two reduplicated or rhyming feet. It is present across
incantations, creature names, place names and effect words, in the mouths of both
speakers, from the first month of the archive to the last:

*mooska-**ka**dooska* · *izza **ka** pizza* · *cat-**ka**-pat* · *slime-**ka**-by* ·
*slime-**ka**bime* · *kamoose-**ka**juice-**ka**boose* · *kibble-**ka**bibble* ·
*bas**ka**madasca* · *as**ka**-baska-noona-maska* · *fiska **ka**piska* ·
*Snooper-**ka**-flooper* · *Floofer **Ka**ploofer* · *Gonk-**a**-Bona* · *cough-**ka**-cough*
· *das**ka** daska*

The rule **[inference]** is: *take a foot, repeat it with a -ka- hinge and an altered
onset.* It is the corpus's default word-formation strategy, and it is doing precisely what
Chukovsky described as the toddler's rhyme appetite — except that here it has been
promoted to the mechanism by which the universe is operated.

A second, narrower pattern is the **-ime/-ime rhyme family**, a phonotactic template
recurring across unrelated spells: *bime, zime, sime, sume, salime, subime, slime,
byme, seem-kabine, slime-kabime*. Ten spell-words in one rhyme class, from six different
stories, is far above chance. **[speculation]** Either *-ime* is the register's unmarked
spell-ending — a kind of derivational suffix for "this is an incantation" — or it is simply
the easiest rhyme in the phonology and everyone reaches for it.

### 5.4 Semantics, and two minimal pairs

The archive contains two genuine minimal pairs, which between them establish where the
meaning of a spell lives.

**Pair 1 — Type B, meaning in the English verb.** Ove demonstrates:

> He held up dice, and he said, "**Mooska-kadooska, disappear!**" And then there was a
> little **floop**, and his hand was empty. […] "we can make the dice come back with the
> same magic word but the word **reappear** after. So ready? **Mooska-kadooska,
> reappear!** **Sloop!** And the dice popped back into existence on his hand."
> — *The Magic of Mooska-Kadooska*, 2025-11-27

Hold *mooska-kadooska* constant, swap *disappear* for *reappear*, and the effect inverts.
The invocation is therefore **semantically empty** — a licence to cast, satisfying the
rhyme requirement and nothing more — and the operative content is carried by an ordinary
English imperative. Ove had just told Seeker he may invent a fresh invocation whenever he
likes, which is only coherent if the invocation means nothing.

**Pair 2 — Type A, meaning in the form itself.** Hattie, walking in the woods and idly
thinking about words, invents one:

> **Izzy:** "Baskamadasca, jiggidy **jaska**."
> **Dad:** And suddenly there was a poof! Because **she didn't know it, but that word that
> she just made up happened to be a magic spell.** And it was the kind of spell that
> summons a creature. […]
> **Izzy:** "Pinecone! Baskamadasca, jiggidy **jeeza**!"
> **Dad:** And so Hattie said her new spell, baskamadasca, jiggidy jeeza, and then poof!
> There was a poof and a pop and a little rustle down on the forest floor because Pinecone
> Mouse was actually a very small mouse.
> — *Hattie's Magic Mouse Summoning*, 2025-07-19

*Baskamadasca jiggidy* is held constant and functions as the operator SUMMON; the final
foot alternates *jaska* ~ *jeeza* and selects the argument — Mischief Mouse, then Pinecone
Mouse. **[inference]** Type A spells are not monoliths after all. This one, at least, is
**operator + argument**, with the argument in final position: exactly the Type B
architecture, but with both slots filled by non-English material.

### 5.5 The effect particles

Every successful spell produces a sound, and these form a small closed class distinct from
the spell itself: *poof, floop, sloop, sploop, bloop, pring, ka-poof, ga-bounce,
kibble-kabibble, floop-then-poof, Bush!, tada*. They are not spoken by anyone. They are
what the world says back.

Two observations. First, they are **phonaesthetically contrastive**: *floop* marks
disappearance, *sloop* marks reappearance, and the /fl/ ~ /sl/ alternation is doing the
semantic work in a pair that is otherwise identical. Second, and remarkably, **they are
subject to correction**:

> **Dad:** "…zong, zing. **Ka-poof.** And Seeker and Isabella—"
> **Izzy:** "No, it was supposed to go **ga-bounce**."
> **Dad:** "…ga-bounce, appeared in a soft, gently glowing landscape."
> — *The Secret of the Galapagos Blub Creatures*, 2026-08-29

The narrator supplies the wrong arrival noise and is overruled. **[inference]** The effect
particle is not decorative and not free: a given potion arriving in a given world makes a
particular sound, that fact is part of the canon, and Izzy is its keeper.

## VI. Onomastics and the lexicon

### 6.1 Cultural keywords

A handful of coinages are not nonsense but **untranslatable cultural terms** — words that
name an institution for which English has no equivalent, introduced by Izzy and accepted
into the world's furniture. The clearest is the *Bissolam*:

> **Izzy:** "The Bissolam. It's the Bissolam. **That's a special day when we dance.**"
> **Dad:** "Oh, the Bissolam is a special day when we dance. **I didn't know that.**"
> **Izzy:** "That's the day when we twirl. […] The Bissolam has very nice music."
> — *Haddy the Mouse and the Sparkly Bissolam*, 2025-03-15

Over the scene the Bissolam accretes music, twirling, crackers, grey chairs and a baby
lounge. It is a festival, defined ostensively by a native informant to an outsider who
says, in as many words, *I didn't know that* — and the direction of that transmission,
from the two-year-old to the adult, is the standard direction for every lexical innovation
in this archive.

Comparable items: the *Songo* feast (a restaurant reachable only by air), the *squerret* (a
foodstuff, elicited by the canonical frame "A squerret? What's that?"), the *Cornebyborn*
(a dance, taught by demonstration), *mookball*, and *floof-berries*.

### 6.2 Coinage as a gift

One usage deserves separate note. The archive's most-cited spell story opens, before any
story has begun, with:

> **Izzy:** "**Daska daska daska for you! It's yours!**"
> **Dad:** "Oh, a made-up word for me?"
> — *Hattie's Magic Mouse Summoning*, 2025-07-19

A word is given as a possession — offered, transferred, owned. **[speculation]** This is
the pragmatic frame that makes the rest of the archive's lexicography work: in this
universe words are *objects that can be made and handed over*, which is why Izzy can
introduce *Zararar* to Frasgle as "a noise for you" (§3.5) and why Seeker's proposal of
*mooska-kadooska* is something a teacher can accept or refuse.

## VII. Metalinguistics: what the speakers believe about language

### 7.1 The arbitrariness problem

The archive contains explicit, incompatible testimony on whether a coined form has an
inherent meaning. Both positions are held by the same speaker, sixteen months apart.

**Against arbitrariness.** Hattie invents *baskamadasca, jiggidy jaska* while merely
"thinking about words", and a creature materialises, because — the narration is emphatic —
"**she didn't know it, but that word that she just made up happened to be a magic
spell.**" A form invented with no intention nonetheless *already had* a meaning. The space
of possible strings is pre-populated; coinage is discovery, not creation. This is a
straightforward denial of the arbitrariness of the sign, and it is load-bearing: it is why
anyone in this cosmos need be careful what they say.

**For arbitrariness.** Four months earlier, aged 2;7, Izzy opens a recording with a
coinage and is asked what it means:

> **Izzy:** "Stars, stories, **brio neo**."
> **Dad:** "Brio neo, what does that mean?"
> **Izzy:** "**That means nothing!** Brrrrt."
> — *Hattie the Mouse's Hat Workshop*, 2025-03-23

And at 3;10, asked what language her invented words belong to: "**Not any kind. It's just
made-up words.**"

The two positions cannot be reconciled by ordering — the sceptical position is the earlier
one and is restated at the end of the corpus. **[inference]** The most economical reading
is that the universe distinguishes **meaningless coinage** (*brio neo*, made-up words,
which are just play) from **inadvertently well-formed spells** (*baskamadasca*), and that
nothing in the archive tells a speaker which they have produced until something appears in
front of them. That is a genuinely frightening theory of language, and it is a
three-year-old's.

### 7.2 Prescriptivism

The archive's authority on correct usage is unambiguous and is not the adult. Izzy
corrects Dad's coinage (*squeakety-squeak* → *Squackety-squeak*), his effect particles
(*ka-poof* → *ga-bounce*), his casting (a rhyme said twice must be said a third time), and
his facts. The world-dna notes record the same pattern outside language proper: "*No, not
blue fur, orange fur*"; "*No, made by Isabella. Silver Cat can't make potions.*"

**[inference]** Every language in this cosmos has exactly one competent authority, and
descriptive linguistics is not available to her interlocutor.

## VIII. Sociolinguistics, biology, and history

### 8.1 The interpreter class

Translation in the archive is a **named professional role**, not an ambient capacity.
Queso Dog is "the ship's official translator". Mole "serves as a bridge between worlds,
translating Frasgle's unique tongue." Bunsen is Beaker's standing interpreter and scribe.
Wobbly Whiskers announces competence when needed — "**I speak cat.** I know what Ginger's
saying." Seeker claims duck.

Above all of them sits Izzy, who is the only character able to interpret *every* language
in the corpus, does so without being asked, and is the sole source of every gloss the
reader receives. **[inference]** The archive has one universal polyglot and she is the
narrator's daughter. Dad, by contrast, is a serial partial-competent: he speaks "a little
bit of mooker", "enough cat language" (in fact, not enough), fluent nyerber-nyerber, and
no meep, no space rock, no blub.

Even Izzy's competence has a floor. Confronted with Beaker's thirteen-syllable turn she
reports honestly: "**I don't know, that long, that long meep meant…**"

### 8.2 Biology

**[speculation, but constrained by §3.1.]** The zoosemiotic languages appear to be
*products of anatomy rather than of culture*. Each species produces the sound its body
makes — mice squeak, cats meow, Mookers mook — and its entire linguistic system is built
by reduplicating and modulating that one sound. This predicts, correctly, that no
zoosemiotic language in the corpus has more than one syllable type; that none has a
writing system (§3.7); that all are learnable by outsiders only imperfectly and by ear
(§3.4); and that the mechanical languages, whose speakers have no body of the relevant
kind, break the pattern (§4.4).

It predicts one thing the archive contradicts. Dragons hatch speaking "baby dragon
language" — "[Dragon voice] He said, and in baby dragon language that means: 'I need some
snacks right away!'" — and then, two turns later, the same newly hatched dragon speaks
fluent English: "I don't know, I was just hatched! You gotta help me!" **[inference]**
Either baby dragon language is a transient neonatal register abandoned within minutes of
hatching, or dragons acquire the matrix language instantaneously. Adult dragonets across
two dozen stories speak English exclusively, which is consistent with either.

### 8.3 History

The archive offers almost no diachrony. The one hint is a character record describing the
cat Silver as "communicating through an **ancient**, rhythmic language of resonant purrs",
and the Mookers as using "a **rhythmic chorus** of 'mooks'" — the word *rhythmic* attaching
independently to two unrelated codes. **[speculation]** If the zoosemiotic languages are
prosodic rather than segmental (§3.2), *rhythmic* is not decoration but the correct
technical description, and "ancient" would then mean *unchanged*, since a language carried
by rhythm alone has very little in it that can drift.

## IX. Open questions

The following are places where the archive says nothing, or says two things.

1. **Does beep exist?** Attested only through Izzy's mishearing of *meep* and Dad's
   improvised rescue ("spoken only by trucks and babies"). A bilabial confusion promoted to
   canon, or a real language with two speaker populations and no recorded utterance.

2. **Does Ladderish rhyme?** §4.1. The ladder cast a spell in its own language and the
   spell worked. If magical efficacy is phonetic, Ladderish must satisfy the rhyme
   requirement, and *ar, zar, zar* is our only sample. If efficacy is semantic, then Ove's
   law — "magic spells must rhyme" — is a parochial rule of the human school and not a
   law of nature at all. Nothing in the archive chooses.

3. **Is the sign arbitrary?** §7.1. Two explicit, contradictory statements from the same
   speaker. Unresolved and possibly unresolvable.

4. **What is the relationship between *Zararar* and *ar, zar, zar*?** §3.5. Two
   non-human codes, eight months apart, sharing a rare shape.

5. **Why *-ime*?** §5.3. Ten spell-words in one rhyme class across six stories. Suffix or
   accident.

6. **What distinguishes [Roar] from [Small roar/grunt]?** §3.4. A contrast that a native
   speaker enforces and the transcription cannot capture. Permanently lost unless the
   original audio is re-examined.

7. **Do the zoosemiotic languages have syntax at all?** This survey has argued they are
   holophrastic (§3.3), but the argument rests entirely on glosses supplied by
   interpreters. No zoosemiotic utterance in the corpus has ever been segmented by anyone.

8. **Who taught Dad nyerber-nyerber?** He speaks it fluently on first contact and offers
   to teach it. The archive never explains, and Dit Darnet never returns.

9. **What did Ginger say?** Forty-seven meows, glossed in seven words. §3.2 argues the
   remainder is affect. It may not be.
