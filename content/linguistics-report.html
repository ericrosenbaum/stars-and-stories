Stars & Stories · transcript archive · descriptive survey

The Languages of the Stars & Stories Universe

Seventeen communicative codes are attested across 208 improvised bedtime stories: animals who speak in one reduplicated syllable, a ladder with a language of its own, and a ritual register whose grammar is a law of physics. This is what the transcripts establish, what can be inferred from them, and what they refuse to settle.

Compiled 2026-08-29 Corpus: 208 stories, 2024-09-20 → 2026-08-29 16,948 turns · 258,901 words 17 codes · 26 incantations
Abstract

Abstract

The Stars & Stories cosmos supports at least seventeen distinct communicative codes, of which English is only the matrix. This paper sorts them into three classes: the zoosemiotic languages (§3), spoken by animals and small creatures and built from a single reduplicated syllable; the mechanical and alien languages (§4), spoken by ladders, robots, rocks and one unclassifiable being called Dit Darnet; and the incantation register (§5), which is nobody's native language but a ritual code whose correct use is a law of physics.

We show that the zoosemiotic languages are holophrastic and radically context-resolved; that utterance length does not map monotonically onto propositional content; and that the incantation register has a genuinely compositional syntax which a character states outright and then demonstrates with a minimal pair. We close with the archive's central unresolved question — whether the sign is arbitrary — on which the corpus contains explicit and irreconcilable testimony from the same speaker.

Evidence Every form quoted below is verbatim from a transcript. Claims that go beyond the transcripts are marked Inference or Speculation. §9 lists what the archive does not say.

§1method

Section oneMethod, and a caution about the record

The corpus is oral. Every text in the archive is a transcription of a recording made in the dark, and no speaker in it has ever seen these words written down.

This has one consequence the reader should keep in front of them throughout: there is no orthographic standard, and apparent spelling variation is evidence about the transcription, not about the language.

The archive spells one dragonet's name Dimitar, Dimatar, Dimetar, Demitar, Dematar and Dibbletarr; the cat school is Catasory, Catasorry, Ginger Catasorry and Cadasori; Frasgle is also Frazgle; Beaker is also Beeker; Grisco is also Griscko and Grisko; Hattie was Haddy for the archive's first eight months. None of this is dialect variation. It is a transcriber — sometimes a human, sometimes a speech-recognition engine — guessing at a sound.

Where this paper gives a phonological argument it therefore rests on rhyme, syllable count and reduplication, which survive transcription, and not on segmental detail, which does not.

A second caution. The archive's two speakers are a father and a daughter aged 2;1 to 3;10, and much of what is presented below as canon was invented seconds before it was uttered. The languages are nonetheless internally consistent to a degree that repays description, and the inconsistencies (§9) are as interesting as the regularities.

§2the ecology

Exactly one language in the entire corpus receives a derived, English-style glottonym — see Ladderish, §4.1.

§3zoosemiotic
languages

Section threeThe zoosemiotic languages

3.1Phonology is determined by the body

The archive states the governing principle outright, in a scene where the mouse Tot asks Izzy what language her invented words belong to.

Tot: “What kind of language is that, Isabella?”
Izzy: “Not any kind. It's just made-up words.”
Dad (as Tot): “Oh, I like made-up words, like squeakety-squeak and squork. […] most of my made-up words are just squeaks 'cause I'm a mouse.” The Stinky Cheese Volcano and the Floating Balloon Sky · 2026-07-21

This is the corpus's theory of articulatory phonetics in one line. A speaker's inventory of possible new words is bounded by the body that makes them: a mouse's coinages are squeaks not because mice lack imagination but because mice are mice. It explains the otherwise striking fact that every zoosemiotic language in Table 1 is built from one syllable, reduplicated — meep, blub, mook, meow, squeak, bzz, bark — while the mechanical languages (§4) are not.

Note also that Izzy immediately corrects Dad's coinage: he offers squeakety-squeak, she returns Squackety-squeak. That is the archive's editorial pattern in miniature; see §7.2.

3.2Length does not encode content

The obvious hypothesis — that a longer utterance carries more meaning — is stated by no one and refuted by the data.

One meep can carry thirteen words; forty-seven meows carry seven. The correlation is not merely weak, it is inverted at the extremes. Inference The reduplication is therefore best analysed as prosodic and affective rather than segmental — it encodes insistence, agitation or duration of feeling, not lexical material. Ginger's twenty-nine-meow turn is not a long sentence; it is a cat who cannot decide what to do today, at length.

Beaker's distress form supports this. Under stress the shape changes rather than merely lengthening: after falling into the Pacific he produces “Me, me, me, me, me, me, meep” and “Me, me, me, me, meep, me, me, me, me, meep” — a run of reduced open syllables closed by a full meep coda. His character record independently describes “a frantic cadence of staccato ‘meeps’ and ‘me-me’ sounds”. Inference Meep has at least two registers, neutral and agitated, distinguished by syllable reduction and codas rather than by segments.

3.3Holophrasis and pragmatic resolution

The same form recurs with wildly different glosses. Frasgle's unmarked [Roar] is translated, within a single story, as “yes”, “okay”, “okay, I'll try that”, “that's an interesting idea”, and “you're right”.

Inference These languages are holophrastic — one form, one whole speech act — and the specific proposition is supplied almost entirely by context and by the interpreter's judgement. They carry illocutionary force (assent, complaint, greeting, distress) reliably; propositional content is reconstructed, not transmitted.

This is why translation is hedged wherever the interpreter is a novice.

Minnie: “Wait a minute. I think I'm starting to understand Frasgle. I think that one meant ‘Yeah, okay, I'd like to have a bath too.’” And Mickey said, “I think that means you're right.” Frasgle and the Sticky Situation · 2025-07-31

Three hedges in two sentences. By contrast Bunsen, Beaker's interpreter of long standing, never hedges — he simply reports. Inference Hedging in this corpus tracks the interpreter's authority, not the language's difficulty.

3.4Frasglish has phonemic contrasts, and they are enforced

The strongest single piece of evidence that these are languages and not merely noises is a correction.

Mole said, “Oh my, I've never met a creature like you. Now, I wonder if I could try to speak your language.” And so you know what Mole said? [Small roar/grunt]. Which wasn't quite right. And so Frasgle corrected Mole. He said, [Roar]. Frasgle and the Sticky Situation · 2025-07-31

A learner produces an approximation; a native speaker judges it ill-formed and supplies the correct token. That is a phonemic contrast being policed. Whatever separates [Small roar/grunt] from [Roar] is contrastive in Frasglish, and the transcription cannot tell us what it is.

3.5Borrowing, observed live

The same story contains what appears to be the only documented case of lexical borrowing in progress anywhere in the archive.

Izzy: “Frasgle, I have a noise for you: Zararar, Zararar!”
Dad: And then Frasgle did something new. […] And you know what he said this time? Zararar roar! Zararar roar! And then Mole turned to Isabella and said, “Isabella, you got Frasgle to say something different! But what does that mean?”
Izzy: “It means… I love you!”
Dad: “Zararar roar!” said the Mole, and Frasgle said it back: “Zararar roar!” And now everyone said I love you to each other. Frasgle and the Sticky Situation · 2025-07-31

Every stage of a loan is here in nine lines: introduction by an outsider; adoption by the recipient; compounding with native material (Zararar + roar, the borrowed stem taking a Frasglish head); semantic assignment by the donor; and community-wide uptake, as Mole and then “everyone” adopt it. The new item does not replace anything — Frasglish had no attested way to say I love you before this — so it fills a genuine lexical gap.

Speculation Zararar is phonologically unlike anything else Izzy coins. Its closest relative in the entire corpus is ar, zar, zar, the sole attested Ladderish (§4.1), recorded eight months later. Two unrelated non-human codes sharing a rare zar-shape is either coincidence, a substrate this survey cannot reach, or evidence that Izzy holds a stable phonaesthetic category for “alien speech”. The archive does not decide.

3.6Relay interpretation, and receptive competence

The most recent story in the archive, recorded on its final day, contains its most sophisticated linguistic architecture.

“We have made an incredible discovery, which is that I am able to translate blub language into meep language. And then Bunsen translates meep language into English.” The Secret of the Galapagos Blub Creatures · 2026-08-29

This is a relay chain — blub → meep → English — with no direct blub–English channel anywhere. It has real consequences in the story: when Blubbert finally speaks, Bunsen notes “It's a process here to translate” before delivering the gloss, and the latency is played for exactly what it is.

The same story draws, precisely, the distinction between receptive and productive bilingualism.

“Beaker has learned how to speak the language of, or at least understand it. He can't speak blub just yet.” […] “He can only meep, not blub.” The Secret of the Galapagos Blub Creatures · 2026-08-29

Beaker comprehends blub and produces only meep. This is the ordinary condition of the heritage bilingual, and the archive gets it right, including the hopeful yet.

3.7Literacy: meep has no script

Dad: “But Beaker only knows meep language.”
Izzy: “No, no. Bunsen helped Beaker write it. Beaker just told him the words in meep. […] and then Bunsen wrote them.” The Secret of the Galapagos Blub Creatures · 2026-08-29

Inference None of the zoosemiotic languages has a native writing system. Every written message in the archive is in English, and every non-English speaker who wishes to write must go through a bilingual scribe. This is a well-attested real-world sociolinguistic pattern, and it arrived here without anyone planning it.

§4mechanical &
alien

Section fourThe mechanical and alien languages

4.1Ladderish, and the problem it creates

The magic flying ladder speaks. Its language is named twice, in two different ways, in consecutive turns.

Izzy: “It talks the ar, zar, zar language.”
Dad: “Also known as Ladderish.” The Case of the Kidnapped Dwarf and the Magic Ladder · 2026-08-16

Ladderish is the only glottonym in the corpus formed with the English language-name suffix -ish. Every other language is named by its own noise (meep, blub, mook) or by its speakers (cat language, elephant language). Ladderish is the one code the characters have bothered to name the way we name languages.

It also creates the deepest theoretical problem in the archive. Eight months earlier, the same ladder cast a spell.

And so they all said the rhyme together, except the ladder, who said the rhyme in ladder language, and then pring, poof, they appeared flying through the sky. The Giant Rescue on Volcano Island · 2025-12-07

The spell worked in translation.

This is not a small detail. §5 shows that incantations are governed by a strict formal requirement — they must rhyme — and yet here a caster produces the spell in a language whose only attested material is ar, zar, zar, and the party teleports normally. Either Ladderish rhymes in ways we cannot see, or magical efficacy is semantic rather than phonetic: what matters is that the caster meant the spell, not that particular sounds left a particular mouth. The archive is silent, and §9 returns to it.

4.2Nyerber-nyerber

Dit Darnet appears in exactly one story and is gone. His language is disyllabic and reduplicating like the zoosemiotic codes, but he is no animal — he is an engineer who repairs a marble run — and Dad flags him as a foreigner rather than a beast.

“Dit Darnet says, nyerber nyerber nyerber. Oh, Dit Darnet doesn't speak our language. He speaks nyerber-nyerber.” Blast Off to the Hundred Acre Wood · 2025-08-14

Two things are notable. First, “our language” — the only place in the corpus where the English speakers frame themselves as a speech community with an outside. Second, the scene contains the archive's only explicit offer of language instruction.

Dad: “Nyerber nyerber nyerber. He said thank you. I speak nyerber-nyerber. Do you want to try?”
Izzy: “No.” Blast Off to the Hundred Acre Wood · 2025-08-14

She declines. She then uses it unprompted moments later — “He he nyerber nyerber nyerber? That's the wire plug and that's the battery” — code-switching into nyerber to put the question and glossing herself in English. She refuses the lesson and speaks the language anyway, which is a fair summary of how she acquires everything else in this archive.

4.3The Mookers, and a root that will not stay a noun

Mooker is the most morphologically productive non-English root in the corpus. From mook the archive derives, without comment, a species noun (the Mookers), a language name (mooker language), an utterance (Mook, mook, mook), place names (the Planet of the Mookers, the mooker cloud), a game (mookball) — and a verb.

the Mookers were all tired out from their big day of playing with the Floofers, and so they decided to mook their way right into bed and mook right to sleep. The Mookers, the Floofers and the Solar Mookball · 2026-03-28

To mook is a verb of motion in the first instance and something like an aspectual auxiliary in the second — mook right to sleep, on the model of drift right to sleep. Inference For the Mookers, mook is not a word in their language; it is the language, functioning as an all-purpose root that takes whatever category the sentence needs. Compare smurf.

Dad claims partial competence — “I do speak a little bit of mooker language myself. Mook, mook, mook. And that means I hope I can help you out” — glossing three syllables as seven words, consistent with §3.2.

4.4Robot

Zigzag the robot is commanded to produce language, and what emerges is not a reduplicated animal syllable but a compound of a machine noise and an English word.

(1)bleep-bloop, flibbertigibbet a made-up word, on request Zigzag the Dancing Robot and the Cornebyborn Dance · 2025-10-31

Flibbertigibbet is a real English word, and Zigzag is the only speaker in the archive to use it. Speculation Robot speech is the corpus's one attested case of a code that is lexically parasitic — taking its material from English rather than from a body — which follows neatly from §3.1: a machine has no species-specific articulatory inventory to draw on, so it borrows.

4.5The crying rocks

Space rock language is attested once, as “Waaaaaa!”, repeated identically when Dad asks for a repetition. Its character record reads: “Sentient, crying entities found floating in space that speak their own unique language.” Neither Dad nor Izzy can translate it. It is the one language in the corpus that goes entirely unglossed, and the only one whose single attested utterance is indistinguishable from crying.

§5the incantation
register

Section fiveThe incantation register

The incantation register is nobody's mother tongue. No one is a native speaker of it; it has no community; it is used only to do things. It is nonetheless the most thoroughly described code in the archive, because in this universe its grammar is a law of physics — and physics gets explained to children.

5.1The rhyme requirement

Izzy states the mechanism directly: “Potions are something you drink and then you say a rhyme and then they teleport you somewhere. That's how they work.” The teacher Ove states the constraint as a rule of the art.

“magic spells must rhyme. But each time you cast one, you can make up a new rhyme if you like.” The Magic of Mooska-Kadooska · 2025-11-27

Both halves matter. Rhyme is obligatory; the particular rhyme is free. This is a formal requirement on the shape of an utterance with no requirement on its content — a combination with few parallels outside verse forms, and none at all in ordinary language, where form is constrained by what you mean. Here you may mean anything you like so long as it chimes.

5.2Three architectures

The 26 incantations recovered from the corpus fall into three structurally distinct types.

Type A — opaque monoliths. Wholly non-English strings with no internal structure the speakers can name: Ruby Dooby Galoob, Izza Ka Pizza, Comoose, Subime, Za bing, zong, zing, Kamoose-kajuice-kaboose, Zip-sip, Salime, sume, sime, sume, bime, Ding-dong cadong-dong, Aska-baska-noona-maska, Slime-ka-by, slime-ka-by, byme.

Type B — bipartite: free invocation + English imperative. The school register, taught by Ove, and the only spell type whose semantics are demonstrably compositional (§5.4).

Type C — transparent English couplets naming the destination.

(2)Higher and higher to the plane of fire chanted, eyes closed, to reach the Plane of Fire The Mischief Moles and the Plane of Fire · 2025-10-07
(3)Planet of the Mookers, we're the Blue-kers said three times, to reach Planet Mooker Wobbly Whisker's Sticky Adventure on Planet Mooker · 2026-05-02

Type C spells say where they are going. They satisfy the rhyme requirement in plain English and are the only incantations whose meaning any English speaker could recover unaided. Inference Type C looks like the register's honest core, with Types A and B as elaborations — but the chronology refuses to cooperate. The unambiguous first attestations run Type A (2025-07-19), Type C (2025-10-07), Type B (2025-11-27); and the two earliest spells of all (Junk and gunk!, 2025-03-11; Mouse jouse, 2025-03-12) are English rhyming pairs that transform an object without naming a destination or supplying an imperative — a shape that never recurs. The register did not evolve from transparency toward opacity or the reverse. All three types coexist to the last recording.

5.3Morphology: the KA-infix

The single most productive morphological process in the corpus is the insertion of a -ka- element between two reduplicated or rhyming feet. It appears across incantations, creature names, place names and effect words, in the mouths of both speakers, from the first month of the archive to the last.

(4)mooska-kadooska · izza ka pizza · cat-ka-pat · slime-ka-by · slime-kabime · kamoose-kajuice-kaboose · kibble-kabibble · baskamadasca · aska-baska-noona-maska · fiska kapiska · Snooper-ka-flooper · Floofer Kaploofer · Gonk-a-Bona · cough-ka-cough · daska daska the KA-infix, across fifteen unrelated coinages

The rule Inference is: take a foot, repeat it with a -ka- hinge and an altered onset. It is the corpus's default word-formation strategy, and it is doing precisely what Chukovsky described as the toddler's rhyme appetite — except that here it has been promoted to the mechanism by which the universe is operated.

A second, narrower pattern is the -ime rhyme family, a phonotactic template recurring across unrelated spells: bime, zime, sime, sume, salime, subime, slime, byme, seem-kabine, slime-kabime. Ten spell-words in one rhyme class, from six different stories, is far above chance. Speculation Either -ime is the register's unmarked spell-ending — a derivational suffix meaning “this is an incantation” — or it is simply the easiest rhyme in the phonology and everyone reaches for it.

5.4Semantics, and two minimal pairs

The archive contains two genuine minimal pairs, which between them establish where the meaning of a spell lives.

Pair 1 — Type B: meaning lives in the English verb

(5a)Mooska-kadooska, disappear! → a little floop; his hand was empty
(5b)Mooska-kadooska, reappear! → Sloop! the dice popped back into existence on his hand The Magic of Mooska-Kadooska · 2025-11-27

Hold mooska-kadooska constant, swap disappear for reappear, and the effect inverts. The invocation is therefore semantically empty — a licence to cast, satisfying the rhyme requirement and nothing more — and the operative content is carried by an ordinary English imperative. Ove had just told Seeker he may invent a fresh invocation whenever he likes, which is only coherent if the invocation means nothing.

Pair 2 — Type A: meaning lives in the form itself

Hattie, walking in the woods and idly “thinking about words”, invents one.

(6a)Baskamadasca, jiggidy jaska summons Mischief Mouse
(6b)Baskamadasca, jiggidy jeeza summons Pinecone Mouse Hattie's Magic Mouse Summoning · 2025-07-19
And suddenly there was a poof! Because she didn't know it, but that word that she just made up happened to be a magic spell. And it was the kind of spell that summons a creature. Hattie's Magic Mouse Summoning · 2025-07-19

Baskamadasca jiggidy is held constant and functions as the operator summon; the final foot alternates jaska ~ jeeza and selects the argument. Inference Type A spells are not monoliths after all. This one, at least, is operator + argument, with the argument in final position: exactly the Type B architecture, but with both slots filled by non-English material.

5.5The effect particles

Every successful spell produces a sound, and these form a small closed class distinct from the spell itself: poof, floop, sloop, sploop, bloop, pring, ka-poof, ga-bounce, kibble-kabibble, Bush!, tada. They are not spoken by anyone. They are what the world says back.

Two observations. First, they are phonaesthetically contrastive: floop marks disappearance, sloop marks reappearance, and the /fl/ ~ /sl/ alternation does the semantic work in a pair that is otherwise identical. Second, and remarkably, they are subject to correction.

Dad: “…zong, zing. Ka-poof. And Seeker and Isabella—”
Izzy: “No, it was supposed to go ga-bounce.”
Dad: “…ga-bounce, appeared in a soft, gently glowing landscape.” The Secret of the Galapagos Blub Creatures · 2026-08-29

The narrator supplies the wrong arrival noise and is overruled. Inference The effect particle is neither decorative nor free: a given potion arriving in a given world makes a particular sound, that fact is part of the canon, and Izzy is its keeper.

§6onomastics &
lexicon

Section sixOnomastics and the lexicon

6.1Cultural keywords

A handful of coinages are not nonsense but untranslatable cultural terms — words naming an institution for which English has no equivalent, introduced by Izzy and accepted into the world's furniture. The clearest is the Bissolam.

Izzy: “The Bissolam. It's the Bissolam. That's a special day when we dance.”
Dad: “Oh, the Bissolam is a special day when we dance. I didn't know that.”
Izzy: “That's the day when we twirl. […] The Bissolam has very nice music.” Haddy the Mouse and the Sparkly Bissolam · 2025-03-15

Over the scene the Bissolam accretes music, twirling, crackers, grey chairs and a baby lounge. It is a festival, defined ostensively by a native informant to an outsider who says, in as many words, I didn't know that — and the direction of that transmission, from the two-year-old to the adult, is the standard direction for every lexical innovation in this archive.

Comparable items: the Songo feast (a restaurant reachable only by air), the squerret (a foodstuff, elicited by the canonical frame “A squerret? What's that?”), the Cornebyborn (a dance, taught by demonstration), mookball, and floof-berries.

6.2Coinage as a gift

One usage deserves separate note. The archive's central spell story opens, before any story has begun, with a word being handed over.

Izzy: “Daska daska daska for you! It's yours!”
Dad: “Oh, a made-up word for me?” Hattie's Magic Mouse Summoning · 2025-07-19

Speculation This is the pragmatic frame that makes the rest of the archive's lexicography work: in this universe words are objects that can be made and handed over, which is why Izzy can introduce Zararar to Frasgle as “a noise for you” (§3.5), and why Seeker's proposal of mooska-kadooska is something a teacher can accept or refuse.

§7metalinguistics

Section sevenWhat the speakers believe about language

7.1The arbitrariness problem

The archive contains explicit, incompatible testimony on whether a coined form has an inherent meaning. Both positions are held by the same speaker, sixteen months apart.

Against arbitrariness. Hattie invents baskamadasca, jiggidy jaska while merely “thinking about words”, and a creature materialises — because, the narration is emphatic, “she didn't know it, but that word that she just made up happened to be a magic spell.” A form invented with no intention nonetheless already had a meaning. The space of possible strings is pre-populated; coinage is discovery, not creation. This is a straightforward denial of the arbitrariness of the sign, and it is load-bearing: it is why anyone in this cosmos need be careful what they say.

For arbitrariness. Four months earlier, aged 2;7, Izzy opens a recording with a coinage and is asked what it means.

Izzy: “Stars, stories, brio neo.”
Dad: “Brio neo, what does that mean?”
Izzy: “That means nothing! Brrrrt.” Hattie the Mouse's Hat Workshop · 2025-03-23

And at 3;10, asked what language her invented words belong to: “Not any kind. It's just made-up words.”

The two positions cannot be reconciled by ordering — the sceptical position is the earlier one and is restated at the end of the corpus. Inference The most economical reading is that the universe distinguishes meaningless coinage (brio neo; made-up words, which are just play) from inadvertently well-formed spells (baskamadasca) — and that nothing tells a speaker which they have produced until something appears in front of them.

That is a genuinely frightening theory of language, and it is a three-year-old's.

7.2Prescriptivism

The archive's authority on correct usage is unambiguous, and it is not the adult. Izzy corrects Dad's coinages (squeakety-squeak → Squackety-squeak), his effect particles (ka-poof → ga-bounce), his casting (a rhyme said twice must be said a third time), and his facts. The archive's developmental notes record the same pattern outside language proper: “No, not blue fur, orange fur”; “No, made by Isabella. Silver Cat can't make potions.”

Inference Every language in this cosmos has exactly one competent authority, and descriptive linguistics is not available to her interlocutor.

§8sociolinguistics
biology · history

Section eightSociolinguistics, biology, and history

8.1The interpreter class

Translation in the archive is a named professional role, not an ambient capacity. Queso Dog is “the ship's official translator”. Mole “serves as a bridge between worlds, translating Frasgle's unique tongue”. Bunsen is Beaker's standing interpreter and scribe. Wobbly Whiskers announces competence when needed — “I speak cat. I know what Ginger's saying.” Seeker claims duck.

Above all of them sits Izzy, who is the only character able to interpret every language in the corpus, does so without being asked, and is the sole source of nearly every gloss the reader receives. Inference The archive has one universal polyglot and she is the narrator's daughter. Dad, by contrast, is a serial partial-competent: he speaks “a little bit of mooker”, “enough cat language” (in fact, not enough), fluent nyerber-nyerber, and no meep, no space rock, no blub.

Even Izzy's competence has a floor. Confronted with Beaker's thirteen-syllable turn she reports honestly: “I don't know, that long, that long meep meant…”

8.2Biology

Speculation — but constrained by §3.1. The zoosemiotic languages appear to be products of anatomy rather than of culture. Each species produces the sound its body makes — mice squeak, cats meow, Mookers mook — and its entire linguistic system is built by reduplicating and modulating that one sound. This predicts, correctly, that no zoosemiotic language in the corpus has more than one syllable type; that none has a writing system (§3.7); that all are learnable by outsiders only imperfectly and by ear (§3.4); and that the mechanical languages, whose speakers have no body of the relevant kind, break the pattern (§4.4).

It predicts one thing the archive contradicts. Dragons hatch speaking “baby dragon language” — “[Dragon voice] He said, and in baby dragon language that means: ‘I need some snacks right away!’” — and then, two turns later, the same newly hatched dragon speaks fluent English: “I don't know, I was just hatched! You gotta help me!” Inference Either baby dragon language is a transient neonatal register abandoned within minutes of hatching, or dragons acquire the matrix language instantaneously. Adult dragonets across two dozen stories speak English exclusively, which is consistent with either.

8.3History

The archive offers almost no diachrony. The one hint is a character record describing the cat Silver as “communicating through an ancient, rhythmic language of resonant purrs”, and the Mookers as using “a rhythmic chorus of ‘mooks’” — the word rhythmic attaching independently to two unrelated codes. Speculation If the zoosemiotic languages are prosodic rather than segmental (§3.2), rhythmic is not decoration but the correct technical description; and ancient would then mean unchanged, since a language carried by rhythm alone has very little in it that can drift.

§9open questions

Section nineOpen questions

The following are places where the archive says nothing, or says two things.

  1. Does beep exist? Attested only through Izzy's mishearing of meep and Dad's improvised rescue (“spoken only by trucks and babies”). A bilabial confusion promoted to canon, or a real language with two speaker populations and no recorded utterance.
  2. Does Ladderish rhyme? (§4.1) The ladder cast a spell in its own language and the spell worked. If magical efficacy is phonetic, Ladderish must satisfy the rhyme requirement, and ar, zar, zar is our only sample. If efficacy is semantic, then Ove's law — “magic spells must rhyme” — is a parochial rule of the human school and not a law of nature at all. Nothing in the archive chooses.
  3. Is the sign arbitrary? (§7.1) Two explicit, contradictory statements from the same speaker. Unresolved and possibly unresolvable.
  4. What is the relationship between Zararar and ar, zar, zar? (§3.5) Two non-human codes, eight months apart, sharing a rare shape.
  5. Why -ime? (§5.3) Ten spell-words in one rhyme class across six stories. Suffix, or accident.
  6. What distinguishes [Roar] from [Small roar/grunt]? (§3.4) A contrast a native speaker enforces and the transcription cannot capture. Permanently lost unless the original audio is re-examined.
  7. Do the zoosemiotic languages have syntax at all? This survey has argued they are holophrastic (§3.3), but the argument rests entirely on glosses supplied by interpreters. No zoosemiotic utterance in the corpus has ever been segmented by anyone.
  8. Who taught Dad nyerber-nyerber? He speaks it fluently on first contact and offers to teach it. The archive never explains, and Dit Darnet never returns.
  9. What did Ginger say? Forty-seven meows, glossed in seven words. §3.2 argues the remainder is affect. It may not be.
